The scenario in this article starts the day that money stops arriving. | | [Read why OpenAI needs fresh money every year →](https://rift-capital.com/en/blog/what-if-openai-went-bankrupt) | | | $278B  OpenAI’s expected cash shortfall through 2030 | $638B  Oracle’s signed revenue, about half from OpenAI | ~$700B  data-centre investment financed by private credit, 2026–28 | | --- | --- | --- | | | THE REAL DANGER  If OpenAI stopped paying, each supplier would first work out what it could still claim. A cloud provider could rent the capacity to someone else. A data-centre owner would still have to pay for running its sites and repaying its loans. Equipment that can be reused keeps its value. A site built for one customer, special hardware or unusual power needs is harder to rent out again. If its equipment is worth less than the loans taken out to buy it, the operator struggles to repay, and its lenders lose money without ever having invested in OpenAI.  The real danger would come if several operators got into trouble at the same time. Lenders would charge more, lend less or stop lending, and data centres still under construction could struggle to get finished, even with customers who keep paying. No bankruptcy is needed for this to happen. Slower AI revenue growth, falling prices for computing power or more capacity than customers would create the same pressure. Operators could keep paying their debts while earning far less than planned. | | [Read how a stall would spread to lenders →](https://rift-capital.com/en/blog/what-if-openai-went-bankrupt) | | WHO CARRIES THE RISK  Behind them are banks and private-credit funds, investment funds that lend directly to companies instead of going through a bank. The Bank of England cited an estimate of about $700 billion of data-centre investment financed this way between 2026 and 2028.
