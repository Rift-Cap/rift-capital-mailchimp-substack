SpaceX’s IPO gave private space a public price. Now the rest of the sector has to prove its valuations hold.

It remains unclear whether buyers will follow at those prices.  Paul Ravignon  ·  October 2026  ·  6 min read | | On June 11, 2026, SpaceX priced its IPO at $135 a share, valuing the company at about $1.77 trillion. By the end of June, Alphabet’s stake, bought a decade earlier, was worth roughly 100 times its cost.  Until June, “space” in the secondary market mostly meant one name. SpaceX now has a daily public price, and investors can use it to value everyone else. A tilde marks a figure the company has not confirmed. | | CEILINGS, NOT ORDERS  The Pentagon has become the anchor customer, much of it through Golden Dome. In April, 12 companies won contracts worth up to $3.2 billion combined to build space-based interceptor prototypes.  Those figures are spending limits. Stoke was selected for a launch programme worth up to $5.6 billion; its first order was $5 million. A funding round lifts a valuation overnight. A government programme can take years to fill it. | | THE PUBLIC MARKET TEST  York Space Systems listed at $34 in January and traded at $8.98 by October, after delayed government orders cut its guidance by nearly a third. Firefly sits about 49% below its IPO price despite revenue up 659%.  Some of 2026’s highest valuations come from rounds not yet confirmed as closed. Their first test may come in the secondary market, before SpaceX’s lock-up ends in December. | | | SpaceX gave private space a price.
